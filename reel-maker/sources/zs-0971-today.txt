Overview
The whole case on one page.
Young workers are running on empty — and still not stopping. In Deloitte's 2026 survey of more than 22,500 Gen Zs and millennials in 44 countries, 71% of Gen Zs said they needed time off because of stress, but only 41% took it. 47% of Gen Zs feel burned out, and 58% regularly feel digital fatigue. The World Health Organization describes burn-out as a result of chronic workplace stress that hasn't been managed — so this is a work problem, not just a personal one.
A simple example: A 24-year-old analyst feels exhausted by Thursday every week. She still has leave days. But her team is short-staffed, she worries it will look like she isn't committed, and work messages follow her onto her phone. So she keeps going — and gets less done.
researcher note · [modeled]
The trend is not all bad: in the same Deloitte data, Gen Zs rating their mental health as good rose from 52% (2025) to 63% (2026), and those stressed most or all of the time fell from 40% to 34%. All figures are self-reported survey answers. The US figures (Pew, Aflac) are not global.
The case in 30 seconds
who bleeds Young employees who need a break but don't take one — and the employers who pay for it in lower output, sick days and people quitting.
the bleed Burnout, lower engagement and lost productivity. Gallup puts global engagement at 20% in 2025, the lowest since 2020.
the gap Companies offer leave and wellness apps, but nothing makes sure people actually stop. Managers rarely see who is running on empty.
the wedge A simple tool for team managers that shows who hasn't had a real break and helps plan cover so they can take one.
the endgame The 'workload and recovery' layer for small and mid-size teams, sold to employers.
the window Now: the data is fresh (Oct 2026) and year-end is when unused leave and next year's budgets are discussed.
